# Governance Claim Boundaries

Organizations need to be careful about what they say their governance process proves.

Using a framework, completing an assessment, running a control, or adopting a policy does not automatically prove that an AI system is safe, compliant, fair, reliable, or fully governed.

## The Claim Boundary Question

For every governance statement, I want to ask:

**What evidence actually supports this claim, and where does that evidence stop?**

That question matters because governance language can become broader than the underlying evidence.

## Common Claim Levels

These are not formal certification terms. They are a practical way to separate different levels of assurance.

### 1. Informed

The organization has considered a framework, standard, principle, or governance concept.

This may support a statement that the work was informed by that source.

It does not prove implementation or conformance.

### 2. Mapped

The organization has compared its controls, processes, or system characteristics to a framework or set of requirements.

This may support a statement that relevant areas were mapped.

It does not prove that every mapped control is implemented or operating effectively.

### 3. Implemented

The organization has put defined controls or governance processes into operation.

This may support a statement that specific governance measures were implemented.

It does not automatically prove that those measures are effective.

### 4. Tested

The organization has evaluated whether defined controls or processes operated as expected.

This can provide stronger assurance, but the claim should still reflect the scope, period, method, and limitations of the testing.

### 5. Independently Assessed

An outside party has reviewed a defined scope using a stated method.

The value of the claim depends on who performed the assessment, what was reviewed, what criteria were used, and what the conclusion actually says.

### 6. Certified or Conformant

These terms should only be used when there is a legitimate basis for them under the relevant certification or conformance process.

An internal review should not be described as certification simply because it was detailed.

## What a Governance Claim Should Include

A defensible claim should make clear:

* What was reviewed
* What period or version was covered
* Which criteria were used
* What evidence was considered
* What level of assurance was actually reached
* What was outside the scope
* What limitations remain

## Example

A broad statement such as:

> This AI system is compliant and fully governed.

may be difficult to defend because it does not identify the standard, scope, evidence, period, or limitations.

A narrower statement such as:

> The organization completed an internal governance review of this use case against its defined ownership, human oversight, evidence, and escalation controls for the current deployment version.

is more precise because it tells the reader what actually occurred.

Even that statement should only be made if the organization has evidence to support it.

## Why Claim Boundaries Matter

Governance is partly about controls. It is also about preventing false confidence.

Overstating assurance creates its own risk because executives, customers, regulators, investors, employees, or partners may rely on a statement that the evidence does not fully support.

The goal is not weaker language. The goal is accurate language.

An organization should be able to explain exactly why it made a governance claim and show the evidence that supports that claim.
